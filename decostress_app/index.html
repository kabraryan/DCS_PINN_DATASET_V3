What decompression sickness is

Decompression sickness — "the bends" — happens when nitrogen absorbed under pressure comes out of solution as bubbles because a diver ascends faster than their tissues can offload it. Here's the sequence, wrapped around the diver at the centre.

1 · Nitrogen dissolves under pressure

At depth the surrounding pressure rises, forcing more of the nitrogen you breathe into your blood and tissues. The deeper and longer you stay, the more you take on.

2 · Tissues fill at different speeds

Blood and the spinal cord saturate fast; joints, fat and slow tissues lag. This tool tracks the real 16 Bühlmann ZHL-16C compartments, half-times from 4 to 635 minutes.

3 · Ascending lets it back out

As pressure drops on the way up, that dissolved nitrogen has to leave. Do it slowly and the gas diffuses out harmlessly through the lungs.

4 · Too fast, and it bubbles

Ascend faster than tissues can offload and the nitrogen leaves solution inside the body — like opening a shaken bottle — forming bubbles in blood and tissue.

5 · Where bubbles lodge

In joints they cause the classic pain ("the bends"); in the spinal cord or brain, neurological injury; in the lungs, the "chokes." Severity depends on where and how many.

6 · Why divers differ

A PFO (heart shunt), dehydration, fatigue, age and fitness are all believed to shift susceptibility. We could not test any of them. The 1,948 real Navy dives record depth, bottom time, ascent time and outcome — and nothing else. The honest answer here is a shrug.

How this actually works

The physics (real, and correct)

Breathe under pressure and your tissues absorb extra nitrogen. Come up faster than they can offload it and the gas leaves solution as bubbles. This tool runs the genuine Bühlmann ZHL-16C table — all 16 compartments, half-times from 4 to 635 minutes, with the published a and b coefficients. Not a simplification: the same table your dive computer runs.

The dial shows tissue loading — the most over-limit compartment, as a multiple of its surfacing M-value. Below 1.0× every tissue is within what the table permits at the surface; above 1.0×, one is not. It behaves exactly as you would expect: slow the ascent or add a stop, and the number falls.

The prediction (weak, and inverted)

We benchmarked that physics against 1,948 real Navy dives with real outcomes. Bühlmann's risk index scored NOT SUPPORTED — ΔAUC +0.007, p = 0.20. So did gradient factors. So did a full Epstein-Plesset bubble-growth model. The physics is right, and it does not predict who gets bent.

The only thing that carries signal is a plain logistic on depth, bottom time and ascent time — and it manages just AUC 0.64 (a coin flip is 0.50). Worse, its dominant term is ascent time, which in Navy data means "this dive needed a lot of decompression." Read as advice it says ascend faster. So it is reported as a finding on this page, and it is deliberately kept off the dial.

Why you should distrust any dive app that shows you a probability. To print a "% chance of DCS" you need real outcomes to calibrate against. We have 1,948 of them, and they support a ranking, weakly — nothing close to a probability. Any tool quoting you a percentage is either calibrated on data it is not showing you, or it invented the number. This one refuses to.

What we set out to build, and what the data said

The premise was reasonable: dive computers run Bühlmann ZH-L16, a deterministic model that gives a hard ceiling but no sense of how likely you are to get hurt, and no way to account for the individual diver. A data-driven probabilistic model should beat that. We built one and tested it honestly against real outcomes. It lost.

This is a negative result, and it was pre-registered: the benchmark's design spec predicted, before any code ran, that no algorithm would reach SUPPORTED. None did. That prediction holding is the point — it means the pipeline was not tuned until something passed.

What moves the risk

Depth raises the nitrogen pressure your tissues chase. Bottom time gives that loading longer to accumulate. Ascent rate is the lever most under a diver's control: come up slowly and tissues offload before you surface. All of that is true of the physics, and the compartment bars show it faithfully.

But when you ask the real outcomes which of these predicts DCS, the answer is humbling. Max depth barely registers (coefficient +0.007). Ascent time dominates — with the wrong sign, because in Navy records a long ascent means a big decompression obligation, not a careful diver. And physiology — the PFO, the hydration, the sleep, the fitness, the age that every diver is told to care about — could not be tested at all, because the 1,948 real dives do not record a single one of them.

Methods & limitations

What is real here. The tissue kinetics are the genuine Bühlmann ZHL-16C table — 16 compartments, published a and b coefficients, real M-values. The fitted model is a logistic on depth, bottom time and ascent time, trained on 1,948 real dives (305 DCS cases) from the US Navy NMRC 99-02 trials. Its skill, AUC 0.64 ± 0.05, is an out-of-sample estimate from nested cross-validation grouped by dive trial — because trials differ in aggressiveness, and pooling them leaks trial identity into the score (that leak is worth +0.044 AUC on its own).

What this is not. It is not a dive planner and it emits no probability. AUC is a ranking. The ~16% DCS rate in these data comes from Navy trials designed to provoke DCS on partially-extracted negatives; a probability derived from them is meaningless for a recreational diver. Every profile you can build in this explorer also sits far outside the training distribution — those dives averaged 127 fsw for 61 minutes.

The honest ceiling. No decompression model is derived from first principles; all are semi-empirical and calibrated to trial outcomes. A more elaborate model is more internally sophisticated, not more validated. Building a real personalised DCS predictor is not blocked on cleverness — it is blocked on data that does not exist: dive logs recording diver physiology alongside outcomes.

Never use this tool to make in-water or ascent decisions. It does not predict decompression sickness — that is its central finding, not a caveat. The one model here that is fitted to real outcomes would, read as advice, tell you to skip your safety stop. Consult your dive computer, your training agency, and a physician for any actual dive planning.
